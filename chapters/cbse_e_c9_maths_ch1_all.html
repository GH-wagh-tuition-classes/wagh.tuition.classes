The Story Behind Coordinates

Coordinate geometry didn't appear from nowhere — it has thousands of years of history rooted right here in Bhārat.

Did you know?

The very first systematic use of grid-based thinking happened in the Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation — city streets were laid out in precise North–South and East–West directions, 10 metres apart. A merchant could find any shop by simply counting grid units — a real coordinate system in practice!

~3000
BCE
Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation Cities built on grid plans with streets running N–S and E–W at uniform distances — the first known use of grid-based coordinates on a large scale.
800
BCE
Baudhāyana Used East–West and North–South lines for geometric constructions; developed the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem — the core tool for the distance formula.
4th c.
BCE
Ujjayinī as Central Meridian Ancient Siddhāntas described Ujjayinī as the zero-longitude reference point from which all other locations were measured — an early origin point!
499
CE
Āryabhaṭa Replaced Greek 'chords' with 'sines', making coordinate calculations far easier. Mapped the sky using Celestial Coordinates.
628
CE
Brahmagupta Formalised zero and negative numbers as algebraic entities — without this, the four-quadrant Cartesian plane would be impossible.
~1100
CE
Ömar Khayyām First mathematician to solve algebraic problems using geometry by interpreting them as coordinates in a plane.
1637
CE
René Descartes Formalised that any point in a 2-D plane can be defined by exactly two numbers — the distances from two perpendicular axes. The Cartesian plane is named after him.

The Basics

Before we can plot anything, we need to understand the structure of the coordinate system.

What is a Coordinate System?

A coordinate system is a structured framework — like the grid lines on graph paper — that lets us use numbers to describe the exact location of any point in space.

The Two Axes

  • x-axis — the horizontal line
  • y-axis — the vertical line
  • The two axes are perpendicular (at right angles) to each other
  • Their point of intersection is called the Origin O, with coordinates (0, 0)

Positive & Negative Directions

  • Right of O → positive x
  • Left of O → negative x
  • Above O → positive y
  • Below O → negative y

Reading Coordinates: P(x, y)

A point P(x, y) tells us:

  • x = perpendicular distance of P from the y-axis (measured along x-axis)
  • y = perpendicular distance of P from the x-axis (measured along y-axis)

Important: The order matters! P(3, 5) and P(5, 3) are different points. If x ≠ y, then (x, y) ≠ (y, x).

Special Points

  • A point on the x-axis has coordinates of the form (x, 0)
  • A point on the y-axis has coordinates of the form (0, y)
  • The origin is (0, 0)

The Four Quadrants

The two coordinate axes divide the plane into four regions called quadrants. Knowing which quadrant a point is in tells you the signs of its coordinates.

Quadrant I

( + , + )

x is positive, y is positive.
Example: (3, 4), (7, 2)

Quadrant II

( − , + )

x is negative, y is positive.
Example: (−5, 3), (−2, 8)

Quadrant III

( − , − )

x is negative, y is negative.
Example: (−4, −6), (−1, −9)

Quadrant IV

( + , − )

x is positive, y is negative.
Example: (5, −3), (2, −7)

Memory Tip 🧠

Go anti-clockwise starting from top-right: I → II → III → IV. Signs go: ++, −+, −−, +−. Think of it as a cycle where x turns negative first (in Q II), then y (in Q III), then x turns positive again (in Q IV).

Points on the Axes

Points that lie exactly on an axis do not belong to any quadrant. For example, (3, 0) is on the x-axis and (0, −4) is on the y-axis — neither is in a quadrant.

The Distance Formula

How do we find the distance between two points that are NOT on the same axis? We use the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem!

The Key Idea

Given two points A(x₁, y₁) and D(x₂, y₂), draw a right-angled triangle by drawing horizontal and vertical lines from each point. The legs of the triangle are:

  • Horizontal leg = |x₂ − x₁| (difference in x-coordinates)
  • Vertical leg = |y₂ − y₁| (difference in y-coordinates)

Then apply the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem to find the hypotenuse — that is the distance AD.

Distance Formula
d = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]
Works for any two points in any quadrant. Negative values are fine — squaring removes the sign.

Textbook Example

Find the distance between A(3, 4) and D(7, 1):

  • Horizontal: 7 − 3 = 4
  • Vertical: 4 − 1 = 3
  • Distance = √(4² + 3²) = √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5 units

This is a classic 3-4-5 Pythagorean triple!

Chapter Summary

All the key points from Chapter 1 at a glance.

📐
Two Perpendicular Lines

The x-axis (horizontal) and y-axis (vertical) form the coordinate system.

🎯
Origin O(0, 0)

The point of intersection of both axes, where all measurements begin.

🧭
Four Quadrants

The axes divide the plane into four quadrants: I(+,+), II(−,+), III(−,−), IV(+,−).

📍
Coordinates (x, y)

x = distance from y-axis; y = distance from x-axis. Order matters!

➡️
Points on Axes

x-axis: form (x, 0). y-axis: form (0, y). Origin: (0, 0).

📏
Distance Formula

d = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²] — from the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem.

↔️
Same-axis Distances

Between (x₁,y) and (x₂,y): |x₂−x₁|. Between (x,y₁) and (x,y₂): |y₂−y₁|.

🪞
Reflection Preserves Distance

When a shape is reflected in an axis, its side lengths remain unchanged.

The Most Important Formula to Remember
d(A, B) = √[ (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² ]
This single formula lets you find the distance between ANY two points in the plane.

  2. Slope AG = (8−0)/(6−0) = 4/3
