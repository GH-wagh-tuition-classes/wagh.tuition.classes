Comprehensive Definitions & Explanations

1. Nature of Acids and Bases

  • Acid: A chemical substance that possesses a sour taste, turns blue litmus paper red, and dissociates to yield hydrogen ions H+(aq) or hydronium ions H3O+ in an aqueous solution.
  • Base: A chemical compound that features a bitter taste, feels soapy to the touch, turns red litmus paper blue, and generates hydroxide ions OH-(aq) when dissolved in water.
  • Alkali: A specific class of bases that are highly soluble in water, such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and potassium hydroxide (KOH).
  • Indicator: A natural or synthetic substance that alters its color or odor depending on whether it is placed in an acidic or basic medium.
  • Olfactory Indicator: An exceptional indicator substance whose characteristic odor changes systematically when mixed with an acid or a base.
  • Synthetic Indicator: Artificially prepared laboratory chemical color indicators, most notably Phenolphthalein and Methyl Orange.

2. Chemical Interactions & The pH Scale

  • Neutralization Reaction: A classic ionic reaction between an acid and a base that produces a salt and water, fundamentally represented as: H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l).
  • pH Scale: A logarithmic scale ranging from 0 to 14 used to specify the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution, tracking the relative concentration of H+ ions.
  • Universal Indicator: A carefully calibrated mixture of several distinct organic indicators that exhibits a smooth spectrum of color variations across the entire pH scale.
  • Strong Acid: An acid that dissociates completely into its constituent ions in an aqueous solution, yielding a high concentration of H+ ions (e.g., HCl, H2SO4).
  • Weak Acid: An acid that only partially ionizes when dissolved in water, resulting in a low concentration of H+ ions in the solution (e.g., CH3COOH).
  • Dilution: The process of mixing an acid or base with water to systematically reduce its concentration of ions (H3O+ or OH-) per unit volume.

3. Salts and Commercial Chemicals

  • Salt: An ionic compound formed by the neutralization reaction between an acid and a base, containing a positive metallic/ammonium ion and a negative acid radical.
  • Chlor-Alkali Process: An industrial electrochemical process involving the electrolysis of an aqueous sodium chloride solution (brine) to yield chlorine gas, hydrogen gas, and sodium hydroxide.
  • Brine: A highly concentrated aqueous solution of sodium chloride (NaCl).
  • Water of Crystallization: The fixed, definite number of water molecules chemically combined in a loose crystalline structure within one formula unit of a salt (e.g., 5H2O in CuSO4·5H2O).
  • Plaster of Paris: A white powdery chemical compound, calcium sulphate hemihydrate (CaSO4·½H2O), obtained by carefully heating gypsum to 373 K.
  • Gypsum: A hard crystalline mineral compound, calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O), formed when Plaster of Paris absorbs water and sets into a solid mass.

Detailed Analysis of Chapter Activities

This section outlines the laboratory experiments for Chapter 2, accompanied by precise structural vector graphics.

Activity 2.1: Testing Acids and Bases with Indicators

Procedure: Collect samples of various laboratory acids and bases on a watch-glass and test them using red litmus, blue litmus, phenolphthalein, and methyl orange indicators.

Observation: Acids turn blue litmus red and stay colorless in phenolphthalein. Bases turn red litmus blue and produce a deep pink color in phenolphthalein.

Activity 2.3: Reaction of Acids with Metal Granules

Procedure: Add zinc granules to dilute sulphuric acid in a test tube, route the escaping gas through a soap solution via a delivery tube, and bring a burning candle near the bubbles.

Observation: Gas bubbles form rapidly on the surface of the zinc pieces. When passing into the soap solution, bubbles rise into the air and burst with a sharp pop sound near a flame.

Inference & Chemical Equation: Active metals displace hydrogen from dilute acids, generating hydrogen gas.

Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)↑

Activity 2.5: Reaction of Metal Carbonates with Acids

Procedure: Take 0.5 g of sodium carbonate in a test tube, add 2 mL of dilute HCl, and route the generated gas into a second test tube containing lime water.

Observation: Brisk effervescence occurs in the first test tube. The lime water in the second tube turns milky due to a white precipitate.

Inference & Chemical Equation: Metal carbonates react with acids to yield carbon dioxide gas, which precipitates calcium carbonate from lime water.

Na2CO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)↑
Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) → CaCO3(s)↓ [Milky Precipitate] + H2O(l)

Activity 2.8: Electrical Conductivity of Acidic Solutions

Procedure: Fix two iron nails on a rubber cork inside a beaker. Connect them to a 6V battery and a bulb. Pour dilute HCl into the beaker and switch on the current.

Observation: The bulb glows brightly with dilute HCl and dilute H₂SO₄, but does not glow with glucose or alcohol solutions.

Conclusion: Acids dissociate into mobile ions in aqueous solution that conduct electricity.

Activity 2.9: Testing Dry vs. Aqueous HCl Gas

Procedure: Add conc. H₂SO₄ to solid NaCl in a test tube. Pass the evolved gas over dry blue litmus paper, then wet blue litmus paper.

Observation: Dry blue litmus paper shows no color change. Wet blue litmus paper turns red instantly.

Conclusion: HCl gas acts as an acid only when moisture is present to form hydronium ions.

NaCl(s) + H2SO4(conc) → NaHSO4(s) + HCl(g)↑

Activity 2.15: Removing Water of Crystallization

Procedure: Heat blue copper sulphate crystals in a dry boiling tube. Observe changes, cool, and add water.

Observation: Upon heating, the blue crystals turn into a dull white powder and water droplets condense near the tube's neck. Adding water restores the blue color.

CuSO4·5H2O(Blue) → CuSO4(White) + 5H2O↑

PART I: SOLUTIONS TO QUESTIONS INSIDE THE CHAPTER

In-Text Questions (Page 18)

Q1: You have been provided with three test tubes. One of them contains distilled water and the other two contain an acidic solution and a basic solution, respectively. If you are given only red litmus paper, how will you identify the contents of each test tube?

Answer:
1. Dip the red litmus paper into each test tube.
2. The solution that turns the red litmus paper blue is the basic solution.
3. Now, divide the blue litmus paper into two pieces and dip them into the remaining two test tubes.
4. The solution that turns the blue litmus paper back to red is the acidic solution.
5. The test tube that shows no color change on the blue litmus paper contains distilled water (neutral).

In-Text Questions (Page 22)

Q1: Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?

Answer: Curd and other sour foods contain naturally occurring organic acids (such as lactic acid). These acids react chemically with metals like copper and brass to form toxic, soluble metal compounds. This corrodes the vessel and causes food poisoning.

Q2: Which gas is usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal? Illustrate with an example. How will you test for the presence of this gas?

Answer: Hydrogen gas (H₂) is usually liberated.
Example: Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)↑
Test: Bring a burning candle near the mouth of the test tube. The evolved hydrogen gas will ignite and burn with a characteristic pop sound.

Q3: Metal compound A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce effervescence. The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle. Write a balanced chemical equation for the reaction if one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride.

Answer: Since the gas extinguishes a fire and causes effervescence, it must be carbon dioxide (CO₂). Since calcium chloride (CaCl₂) is a product, Compound A must be Calcium Carbonate (CaCO₃).
Equation: CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)↑

In-Text Questions (Page 25)

Q1: Why do HCl, HNO₃, etc., show acidic characters in aqueous solutions while solutions of compounds like alcohol and glucose do not show acidic character?

Answer: HCl and HNO₃ completely dissociate into hydrogen ions (H⁺) or hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) in water. These free ions are responsible for their acidic properties. Covalent compounds like glucose and alcohol do not ionize in water to release H⁺ ions.

Q2: Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?

Answer: An acid dissociates into mobile cations (H⁺) and anions in an aqueous solution. These free-moving ions act as charge carriers that conduct electric current.

Q3: Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of the dry litmus paper?

Answer: HCl gas only dissociates into its acidic H⁺ ions in the presence of water. Without moisture, no free ions are formed to change the indicator's color.

Q4: While diluting an acid, why is it recommended that the acid should be added to water and not water to the acid?

Answer: Diluting concentrated acid is highly exothermic. If water is poured directly into acid, the sudden massive heat generation can cause the mixture to flash-boil and splash, causing severe burns. Adding acid slowly to a large volume of water dissipates the heat safely.

Q5: How is the concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?

Answer: Dilution increases the overall volume of the solution, which causes the number of hydronium (H₃O⁺) ions per unit volume to decrease.

Q6: How is the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH⁻) affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?

Answer: Dissolving excess base releases more OH⁻ ions, increasing the number of hydroxide ions per unit volume and making the solution more concentrated.

In-Text Questions (Page 28)

Q1: You have two solutions, A and B. The pH of solution A is 6 and pH of solution B is 8. Which solution has more hydrogen ion concentration? Which of this is acidic and which one is basic?

Answer: Lower pH indicates a higher H⁺ concentration. Solution A (pH=6) has a higher hydrogen ion concentration. Solution A is acidic, and Solution B is basic.

Q2: What effect does the concentration of H⁺(aq) ions have on the nature of the solution?

Answer: A higher concentration of H⁺ ions lowers the pH value, making the solution more strongly acidic.

Q3: Do basic solutions also have H⁺(aq) ions? If yes, then why are these basic?

Answer: Yes, they have H⁺ ions from the self-ionization of water. They are basic because the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH⁻) heavily outweighs the concentration of H⁺ ions.

Q4: Under what soil condition do you think a farmer would treat the soil of his fields with quick lime, slaked lime or chalk?

Answer: A farmer uses these basic compounds when the soil is too acidic. The bases neutralize the excess soil acidity to restore an optimal pH for crops.

In-Text Questions (Page 33)

Q1: What is the common name of the compound CaOCl₂?

Answer: Bleaching powder.

Q2: Name the substance which on treatment with chlorine yields bleaching powder.

Answer: Dry slaked lime or calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂.

Q3: Name the sodium compound which is used for softening hard water.

Answer: Sodium carbonate (Washing Soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).

Q4: What will happen if a solution of sodium hydrocarbonate is heated? Give the equation.

Answer: It decomposes to form sodium carbonate, water vapor, and carbon dioxide gas.
Equation: 2NaHCO3(aq) → Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)↑

Q5: Write an equation to show the reaction between Plaster of Paris and water.

Answer: CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O

PART II: SOLUTIONS TO EXERCISES AT THE END OF THE CHAPTER

Chapter End Exercises (Pages 34-36)

Q1: A solution turns red litmus blue, its pH is likely to be: (a) 1 (b) 4 (c) 5 (d) 10

Answer: (d) 10. Basic solutions turn red litmus paper blue, and bases have pH values above 7.

Q2: A solution reacts with crushed egg-shells to give a gas that turns lime-water milky. The solution contains: (a) NaCl (b) HCl (c) LiCl (d) KCl

Answer: (b) HCl. Eggshells are made of CaCO₃. It reacts with HCl to release CO₂ gas, which turns lime water milky.

Q3: 10 mL of a solution of NaOH is found to be completely neutralised by 8 mL of a given solution of HCl. If we take 20 mL of the same solution of NaOH, the amount HCl solution required to neutralise it will be: (a) 4 mL (b) 8 mL (c) 12 mL (d) 16 mL

Answer: (d) 16 mL. (Volume doubles proportionally).

Q4: Which one of the following types of medicines is used for treating indigestion? (a) Antibiotic (b) Analgesic (c) Antacid (d) Antiseptic

Answer: (c) Antacid.

Q5: Write word equations and then balanced equations for the reaction taking place when —
(a) dilute sulphuric acid reacts with zinc granules.
(b) dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with magnesium ribbon.
(c) dilute sulphuric acid reacts with aluminium powder.
(d) dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with iron filings.

Answer:
(a) Zinc + Dilute Sulphuric Acid → Zinc Sulphate + Hydrogen
Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)↑
(b) Magnesium + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid → Magnesium Chloride + Hydrogen
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)↑
(c) Aluminium + Dilute Sulphuric Acid → Aluminium Sulphate + Hydrogen
2Al(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2(g)↑
(d) Iron + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid → Iron(II) Chloride + Hydrogen
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + H2(g)↑

Q6: Compounds such as alcohols and glucose also contain hydrogen but are not categorised as acids. Describe an Activity to prove it.

Answer: Set up a circuit with a 6V battery, a bulb, and two nails in a beaker. When dilute HCl is added, the bulb glows because HCl provides free ions. However, if glucose or alcohol solution is added, the bulb does not glow. This proves they do not dissociate into H⁺ ions in water, so they are not acids.

Q7: Why does distilled water not conduct electricity, whereas rain water does?

Answer: Distilled water is pure and has no dissolved free ions. Rainwater picks up atmospheric gases like CO₂ and SO₂, forming weak acids (like carbonic acid) that dissociate into free ions, enabling electrical conduction.

Q8: Why does dry HCl gas not show acidic behaviour in the absence of water?

Answer: Acidic behavior is strictly caused by free H⁺ or H₃O⁺ ions. HCl gas can only dissociate into these ions when dissolved in water.

Q9: Five solutions A, B, C, D and E when tested with universal indicator showed pH as 4, 1, 11, 7 and 9, respectively. Which solution is (a) neutral? (b) strongly alkaline? (c) strongly acidic? (d) weakly acidic? (e) weakly alkaline? Arrange the pH in increasing order of hydrogen-ion concentration.

Answer:
(a) Neutral: D (pH 7)
(b) Strongly alkaline: C (pH 11)
(c) Strongly acidic: B (pH 1)
(d) Weakly acidic: A (pH 4)
(e) Weakly alkaline: E (pH 9)
Increasing order of H⁺ concentration: C (11) < E (9) < D (7) < A (4) < B (1).

Q10: Equal lengths of magnesium ribbons are taken in test tubes A and B. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is added to test tube A, while acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is added to test tube B. In which test tube will the fizzing occur more vigorously and why?

Answer: Fizzing occurs more vigorously in test tube A. HCl is a strong mineral acid that fully ionizes, producing a high concentration of H⁺ ions. Acetic acid is a weak acid with fewer H⁺ ions. The higher H⁺ concentration in tube A drives a faster reaction.

Q11: Fresh milk has a pH of 6. How do you think the pH will change as it turns into curd? Explain your answer.

Answer: The pH will decrease (drop below 6). Bacteria ferment the lactose sugar in milk into lactic acid. The presence of this newly formed acid increases the H⁺ ion concentration, lowering the pH.

Q12: A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. (a) Why does he shift the pH of the fresh milk from 6 to slightly alkaline? (b) Why does this milk take a long time to set as curd?

Answer:
(a) The alkaline baking soda neutralizes any naturally forming lactic acid, delaying the souring process and keeping the milk fresh longer.
(b) The bacteria must first generate enough lactic acid to neutralize the added baking soda before the pH can drop low enough to coagulate the milk proteins into curd.

Q13: Plaster of Paris should be stored in a moisture-proof container. Explain why?

Answer: Plaster of Paris easily absorbs ambient moisture from the air. Upon contact with water, it undergoes a rigid hydration reaction, setting into a hard solid mass of gypsum, rendering the powder useless.

Q14: What is a neutralisation reaction? Give two examples.

Answer: A reaction where an acid and a base chemically cancel each other out to form a salt and water.
Example 1: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
Example 2: H₂SO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

Q15: Give two important uses of washing soda and baking soda.

Answer:
Washing Soda: 1) Used to permanently remove water hardness. 2) Used in the glass, soap, and paper industries.
Baking Soda: 1) Used as an active antacid to neutralize stomach acid. 2) Used to make baking powder for spongy cakes and bread.
